<DropCap>
I was shown a school's new AI policy last term with real pride. It had taken a committee four months. It was thorough, carefully worded, and unanimously ratified by the board the week before I visited. It was also, by the time I read it, already wrong — a more capable model had shipped in the interim, and three of the policy's central assumptions no longer held.
</DropCap>

Nobody had done anything wrong. They had written a careful document about a moving target and then, understandably, treated the document as finished. That is the mistake I see most often now, and it is not a mistake of effort or intelligence. It is a mistake about what kind of thing an AI policy is.

<Callout tone="key">
The test of an AI policy was never whether it is right today. It is whether it bends when the ground moves — because the ground will move again next quarter.
</Callout>

## Why good policies go brittle

A policy goes brittle for one of two reasons, and usually both.

The first is that it was written before the people bound by it had any fluency. A policy drafted by a committee that hasn't spent real hours using AI is a set of rules about distinctions its authors have not yet had to make. It reads well and enforces poorly, because it describes a practice that doesn't exist yet.

The second is that it was bound to a moment — a specific tool, a specific set of capabilities, a specific set of fears. The moment passes. The tool is superseded, the capability that was impossible becomes trivial, and the policy that was pinned to that moment snaps.

Both failures share a root: the policy was built to be *correct*, and correctness has a shelf life measured in weeks now.

The alternative isn't a vaguer policy. It's a policy built to bend — one whose durable parts are the parts that don't depend on which model shipped this morning.

Flip that switch a few times and the pattern is hard to unsee. The platform-first school isn't careless — it's just anchored to something that keeps moving out from under it, so it pays the rewrite tax again and again. The program-first school faces the exact same events and absorbs them, because what it committed to — how it wants students to learn, and a shared language for judging AI use — doesn't expire when a model does.

## What "built to bend" actually looks like

Durability here is not vagueness. A living policy is often *more* specific than a brittle one, not less. The difference is where the specificity lives.

- **It commits to learning outcomes, not tools.** The tool is an implementation detail, chosen to serve the outcome and swapped without ceremony when a better one appears.
- **It rests on a shared language.** A faculty that can place any new case into Red, Amber, or Green in under a minute doesn't need the policy to have anticipated that case. The language does the work the document can't.
- **It was written after practice, not before it.** The durable clauses are the ones teachers wrote from six months of real use — because those describe judgments people are already making, not rules they've been handed.
- **It has a version number and a review date.** A living document is one somebody owns, revisits on a schedule, and expects to change. A policy with no next review date has already begun to rot.

None of that is exotic. It's the ordinary discipline of treating guidance as practice rather than as a monument.

<Sidebar label="Field test" title="Is your policy built to bend?">
Three questions that tend to expose a brittle policy fast:

- **When did your faculty last change it — and is the next change already scheduled?** "Never" and "no" are the same answer.
- **Could a new teacher, handed a use you didn't foresee, place it themselves in a minute?** If only the policy's authors can adjudicate, you have a bottleneck, not a policy.
- **If your current AI tool vanished tomorrow, how much of the document would you have to rewrite?** The more the answer approaches "most of it," the more you wrote about a tool and the less you wrote about learning.
</Sidebar>

I want to be careful not to make this sound like a counsel of endless revision. The point isn't to rewrite constantly. It's the opposite: to write the durable things once — the outcomes, the shared language, the ownership — so that the disposable things can change as often as they need to without touching the core.

That is what people mean, or should mean, when they say AI guidance is a living document rather than a policy written once and filed away. Not that it's perpetually unfinished. That it's alive: owned, revisited, and built from the start to bend rather than break. The schools that internalise this will spend the next three years adjusting a paragraph here and there. The ones that don't will spend it rewriting the whole thing, twice, and wondering why the document never seems to hold.
